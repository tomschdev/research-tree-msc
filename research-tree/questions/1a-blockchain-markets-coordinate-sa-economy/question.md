# Rationale

Blockchain-based markets are claimed, theoretically and in places empirically, to coordinate
economic activity. The mechanism runs from shared trust to programmable rules, then to incentives
through value transfer, and finally to coordination among participants who don't know each other.
This MSc must first establish that claim from the literature and the empirical record. It then asks
how the mechanism can be applied in South Africa.

The target is not every South African market. It is the highest-impact setting: a fragmented
economy where coordination fails because of information asymmetry. The clearest example is between
employers and job-seekers, where differences in language, culture and history widen the gap.
Another is the informal economy, where entrepreneurs and citizens lack the shared institutions that
would let them coordinate.

The approach is case-based. Each case puts the theory into a specific context and problem. Each
case is compared with the strongest non-blockchain alternative, not only with the status quo, so
that any gain is attributed to blockchain-specific properties rather than to digitisation alone.
Generalisation is analytic: the cases test the theory's mechanism, not the average South African
market. The "how" is answered in the Finding: which mechanisms, under which conditions, for which
problems.

What each candidate conclusion means here:

- **supported**: the blockchain coordination claim is established from theory and empirical
  literature. In the selected South African case(s), a blockchain-based market mechanism measurably
  improves coordination over the strongest non-blockchain alternative, using the measures
  pre-registered in each case's own question. The improvement is traced to the theorised mechanism,
  and the enabling conditions are identified.
- **refuted**: either the coordination claim does not hold up in the literature, or in the selected
  case(s) the blockchain mechanism gives no coordination gain over the non-blockchain alternative.
  That would be because the theorised mechanism doesn't operate in the context, or because South
  Africa-specific barriers (cost, connectivity, regulation, usability, trust in the technology
  itself) cancel it out.
